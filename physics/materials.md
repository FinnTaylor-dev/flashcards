Q: What are tensile forces?
A: Forces acting away from the centre of a spring or material in both directions, causing it to stretch (extension).

---

Q: What are compressive forces?
A: Forces acting towards the centre of a spring or material in both directions, causing it to shorten (compressive deformation).

---

Q: State Hooke's law.
A: For a material within its elastic limit, the force applied is directly proportional to the extension of the material.

---

Q: What is the mathematical formula for Hooke's law?
A: $F = kx$

---

Q: What does the force constant, $k$, represent in Hooke's law?
A: A measure of the stiffness of a material.

---

Q: What are the units of the force constant, $k$?
A: $N m^{-1}$

---

Q: What happens to a material when the applied force exceeds its elastic limit?
A: Hooke's law is no longer obeyed, and the material undergoes plastic deformation, meaning it will not return to its original shape.

---

Q: In a force-extension graph for a spring, what does the gradient of the initial straight-line section represent?
A: The force constant, $k$.

---

Q: What is elastic deformation?
A: Deformation where the material returns to its original shape when the applied force is removed.

---

Q: What is plastic deformation?
A: Permanent deformation where the material does not return to its original length when the applied force is removed.

---

Q: Describe the force-extension behavior of a metal wire.
A: It obeys Hooke's law and shows elastic deformation up to its elastic limit, with the loading and unloading curves being the same. Beyond this, it experiences plastic deformation, leaving a permanent extension.

---

Q: Describe the force-extension behavior of rubber.
A: It does not obey Hooke's law and does not experience plastic deformation. The area between its loading and unloading curves forms a hysteresis loop, representing energy transferred to thermal energy.

---

Q: Describe the force-extension behavior of polythene.
A: It is a polymeric material that does not obey Hooke's law and experiences plastic deformation as soon as any force is applied, making it easy to stretch into new shapes.

---

Q: How can error be reduced when measuring extension in a force-extension experiment?
A: By reading the values at eye-level and using a set square to ensure the ruler is straight.

---

Q: What is the formula for the total force constant, $k_{total}$, of springs connected in series?
A: $\frac{1}{k_{total}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} + \dots$

---

Q: What is the formula for the total force constant, $k_{total}$, of springs connected in parallel?
A: $k_{total} = k_1 + k_2 + \dots$

---

Q: What happens to the work done when a material is deformed elastically?
A: It is stored as elastic potential energy and released when the material returns to its original length.

---

Q: How can the elastic potential energy stored in a material be calculated from a force-extension graph?
A: By finding the area under the force-extension graph.

---

Q: What are the two formulas for calculating elastic potential energy, $E$, in a material obeying Hooke's law?
A: $E = \frac{1}{2}Fx$ and $E = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$

---

Q: What happens to the work done when a material undergoes plastic deformation?
A: It is not stored as elastic potential energy; instead, it is used to rearrange the atoms into their new permanent positions.

---

Q: Define tensile stress.
A: The force applied to a material per unit cross-sectional area.

---

Q: What is the formula for tensile stress, $\sigma$?
A: $\sigma = \frac{F}{A}$

---

Q: What are the units of tensile stress?
A: $N m^{-2}$ or pascal (Pa).

---

Q: Define tensile strain.
A: The extension or compression of a material per unit of its original length.

---

Q: What is the formula for tensile strain, $\epsilon$?
A: $\epsilon = \frac{x}{L}$

---

Q: Does tensile strain have a unit?
A: No, it is dimensionless (sometimes written as a percentage).

---

Q: Define the Young modulus of a material.
A: The ratio of stress to strain, representing the gradient of the straight-line section of a stress-strain graph.

---

Q: What is the formula for the Young modulus, $E$?
A: $E = \frac{\text{Stress}}{\text{Strain}} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}$

---

Q: What does the Young modulus measure, and is it dependent on the shape or size of the material?
A: It measures the material's stiffness and depends only on the material, independent of its shape and size.

---

Q: Why should the diameter of a wire be measured at several points and averaged when determining the Young modulus?
A: To reduce error and accurately calculate the average cross-sectional area of the wire.

---

Q: In a Young modulus experiment, how is the force applied to the wire calculated?
A: Using $F = mg$, where $m$ is the mass of the slotted masses and $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity.

---

Q: How is the Young modulus determined from experimental data of force and extension?
A: By calculating the stress and strain for each mass, plotting them on a graph, and finding the gradient of the straight-line section.

---

Q: On a stress-strain graph, what does point P represent?
A: The limit of proportionality, up to which Hooke's law is obeyed.

---

Q: On a stress-strain graph, what does point E represent?
A: The elastic limit, beyond which the wire will experience plastic deformation.

---

Q: On a stress-strain graph, what do points Y1 and Y2 represent?
A: The yield points, where there is rapid extension of the material.

---

Q: What is the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of a material?
A: The maximum breaking stress that can be applied to the wire.

---

Q: What characteristic do strong materials have regarding their ultimate tensile strength?
A: They have a high UTS.

---

Q: Describe the stress-strain behavior of a brittle material like glass.
A: It shows elastic behavior until the breakpoint where it snaps, with no plastic deformation and identical loading and unloading curves.

---

Q: Describe the stress-strain behavior of an elastic material like rubber.
A: It can endure a lot of tensile stress before breaking with no plastic deformation, but the unloading curve differs from the loading curve due to energy lost as thermal energy (hysteresis).

---

Q: Describe the stress-strain behavior of a ductile material.
A: It can be easily hammered into thin sheets or drawn into wires, generally experiencing elastic deformation until the elastic limit, followed by plastic deformation before reaching its UTS and breakpoint.

---

Q: What is a hysteresis loop in the context of a force-extension graph for rubber?
A: The area between the loading and unloading curves, representing the energy transferred to thermal energy when the force is removed.

---

Q: How can the force constant of a material be determined from a force-extension graph?
A: By finding the gradient of the straight section of the line, within the elastic limit of the material.

---

Q: What is a fiducial marker used for in a force-extension or Young modulus experiment?
A: To mark the original length of the material or wire on a ruler, allowing for accurate measurement of extension.
