Q: What does Kirchhoff’s second law state?
A: In any closed loop in a circuit, the sum of the electromotive forces (e.m.f.) is equal to the sum of the potential differences (p.d.).

---

Q: What fundamental principle is Kirchhoff’s second law a result of?
A: The conservation of energy.

---

Q: In a series circuit, how does the current behave at every point?
A: The current is the same at every point in the circuit.

---

Q: How is the total electromotive force (e.m.f.) distributed in a series circuit?
A: The e.m.f. is split so that the total potential difference across each component is equal to the e.m.f.

---

Q: What is the formula for the total resistance, $R_t$, of resistors connected in series?
A: $R_t = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$

---

Q: In a parallel circuit, how does the current behave across the different loops?
A: The current in each loop adds up to the total current.

---

Q: In a parallel circuit, how does the potential difference behave across each loop?
A: The potential difference across each loop is the same.

---

Q: What is the formula for the total resistance, $R_t$, of resistors connected in parallel?
A: $\frac{1}{R_t} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots$

---

Q: What is 'lost volts' in the context of a power supply?
A: The difference between the measured potential difference across the terminals of the power supply and the actual e.m.f. of the cell.

---

Q: What causes 'lost volts' in a circuit?
A: The internal resistance of the cell, which transfers some energy to the charge carriers that is not available to the external circuit.

---

Q: How can the internal resistance of a cell be modelled in a circuit diagram?
A: As a source of e.m.f. $\varepsilon$ in series with its internal resistance $r$.

---

Q: What is the equation relating e.m.f. ($\varepsilon$), current ($I$), external resistance ($R$), and internal resistance ($r$)?
A: $\varepsilon = I(R + r)$

---

Q: In the equation $\varepsilon = I(R + r)$, what does the term $IR$ represent?
A: The terminal potential difference ($V$) of the cell.

---

Q: In the equation $\varepsilon = I(R + r)$, what does the term $Ir$ represent?
A: The 'lost volts', which is the potential difference across the internal resistance.

---

Q: How can the internal resistance equation be rearranged to solve for the terminal potential difference ($V$)?
A: $V = \varepsilon - Ir$

---

Q: Describe the experimental circuit setup used to determine the internal resistance of a cell.
A: The cell is connected in series with an ammeter and a variable resistor, and a voltmeter is connected in parallel across the cell.

---

Q: When plotting a graph of terminal potential difference ($V$) against current ($I$) to find internal resistance, what does the y-intercept represent?
A: The e.m.f. ($\varepsilon$) of the cell.

---

Q: When plotting a graph of terminal potential difference ($V$) against current ($I$) to find internal resistance, what does the negative gradient represent?
A: The internal resistance ($r$) of the cell.

---

Q: According to Kirchhoff’s second law, how does the potential difference split in a series circuit with two components?
A: It splits in a ratio proportional to the resistance of each component, such that $\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}$.

---

Q: What is the formula for the output potential difference ($V_{out}$) across a resistor $R_2$ in a potential divider circuit with input voltage $V_{in}$ and resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ in series?
A: $V_{out} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \times V_{in}$

---

Q: In a potential divider circuit, if the resistance of the output resistor ($R_2$) is decreased in proportion to the total circuit resistance, what happens to the output potential difference ($V_{out}$)?
A: The output potential difference ($V_{out}$) decreases.

---

Q: What types of components are typically used in a potential divider circuit to vary the output based on environmental factors?
A: Sensors such as thermistors or light-dependent resistors (LDRs).

---

Q: If a thermistor is used as the output resistor ($R_2$) in a potential divider circuit, what happens to the output potential difference when the temperature decreases?
A: A greater potential difference is provided because the resistance of the thermistor increases.

---

Q: Why does the output potential difference increase when a thermistor's temperature decreases in a potential divider circuit?
A: Because the thermistor's resistance increases, taking a larger proportion of the total circuit resistance and thus a larger share of the input potential difference.
