Q: What is a progressive wave?
A: An oscillation that travels through matter or a vacuum, transferring energy from one place to another without transferring matter.

---

Q: What is transferred by a progressive wave, and what is not transferred?
A: Energy is transferred, but matter is not transferred.

---

Q: In a transverse wave, how do the oscillations relate to the direction of wave travel?
A: The oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of wave travel.

---

Q: Give two examples of transverse waves.
A: Electromagnetic waves and waves on the surface of water.

---

Q: In a longitudinal wave, how do the oscillations relate to the direction of energy transfer?
A: The oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

---

Q: What are the two characteristic regions of a longitudinal wave?
A: Compressions, where particles are closer together, and rarefactions, where particles are more spread out.

---

Q: What is the wavelength ($\lambda$) of a wave?
A: The minimum distance between two adjacent points on a wave oscillating in phase.

---

Q: What is the period ($T$) of a wave?
A: The time taken for a full oscillation of one wavelength to pass a given point.

---

Q: What is the frequency ($f$) of a wave?
A: The number of complete oscillations passing a given point per unit time.

---

Q: What is the mathematical relationship between frequency ($f$) and period ($T$)?
A: $f = \frac{1}{T}$.

---

C: The wave equation states that wave speed ($v$) is equal to the product of [frequency] ($f$) and [wavelength] ($\lambda$), expressed as [$v = f\lambda$].

---

Q: What is phase difference?
A: The difference in displacement of particles along a wave, or in two different waves, measured in radians.

---

Q: What is the phase difference between two particles oscillating in step with each other?
A: A multiple of $2\pi$ radians.

---

Q: What is the formula to calculate the phase difference between two particles separated by a distance $x$ on a wave of wavelength $\lambda$?
A: $\text{Phase difference} = \frac{x}{\lambda} \times 2\pi$.

---

Q: What does it mean for two particles to be in antiphase?
A: They are oscillating with a phase difference of $\pi$ radians, meaning they are half a wavelength ($\frac{\lambda}{2}$) out of step with each other.

---

Q: How can an oscilloscope be used to determine the frequency of a wave?
A: A signal is fed to the oscilloscope, the timebase is set on the x-axis to represent time, the time for one full oscillation is measured to find the period, and frequency is calculated using $f = \frac{1}{T}$.

---

Q: What happens to a wave during reflection?
A: It changes direction at a boundary between two media and remains in the original medium.

---

Q: During reflection, what happens to the angle of incidence, wavelength, and frequency?
A: The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and both the wavelength and frequency remain the same.

---

Q: What causes refraction in a wave?
A: A change in the wave's speed as it enters a new medium, which causes it to change direction.

---

Q: During refraction, which wave properties remain constant and which change?
A: Frequency remains constant, while speed and wavelength change.

---

Q: How does the speed of sound waves and electromagnetic waves change when entering a denser material?
A: Sound waves speed up, whereas electromagnetic waves slow down.

---

Q: What is diffraction?
A: The spreading out of a wave front as it passes through a gap.

---

Q: Under what condition does maximum diffraction occur?
A: When the gap the wave passes through is the same size as the wavelength of the incident wave.

---

Q: What wave properties remain unaltered during diffraction?
A: Wavelength and frequency.

---

Q: Which type of wave can experience polarisation, and why?
A: Transverse waves, because their oscillations occur in many planes at right angles to the direction of travel, allowing them to be restricted to one plane.

---

Q: Why can longitudinal waves not experience polarisation?
A: Because the direction of energy transfer is already in one plane only.

---

Q: How can the polarisation of visible light be demonstrated?
A: By placing two polarising filters on top of each other and rotating one by 90°, which decreases the light intensity to a minimum as the perpendicular polarisation blocks the light.

---

Q: How can the polarisation of microwaves be demonstrated using a metal grille?
A: Vertically plane polarised microwaves pass through a vertically oriented metal grille, but when the grille is rotated to the horizontal orientation, the signal falls to a minimum because the microwaves are absorbed by free electrons in the metal bars.

---

Q: What is the definition of the intensity of a progressive wave?
A: The radiant power passing at right angles through a surface per unit area.

---

Q: What is the formula for the intensity ($I$) of a progressive wave?
A: $I = \frac{P}{A}$, where $P$ is power and $A$ is area.

---

Q: What are the SI units of wave intensity?
A: Watts per metre squared ($\text{W m}^{-2}$).

---

Q: How does the intensity of light from a point source relate to the distance ($r$) from the source?
A: Intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance ($I \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$).

---

Q: How is the intensity of a wave related to its amplitude ($A$)?
A: Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude ($I \propto A^2$).

---

Q: What are the two oscillating fields that make up an electromagnetic wave?
A: Electric and magnetic fields oscillating at right angles to each other.

---

Q: What is the speed of all electromagnetic waves in a vacuum?
A: $3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$.

---

Q: What is the refractive index ($n$) of a medium?
A: The ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum ($c$) to the speed of light in that medium ($v$).

---

Q: What is the formula for the refractive index ($n$)?
A: $n = \frac{c}{v}$.

---

C: [Snell's Law] relates the refractive indices and angles of incidence and refraction relative to the normal, expressed as [$n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$].

---

Q: What are the two conditions required for total internal reflection to occur?
A: The light must travel from a material with a higher refractive index to one with a lower refractive index, and the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle.

---

Q: What is the formula for the critical angle ($C$) when light travels from a material of refractive index $n$ into air?
A: $\sin C = \frac{1}{n}$.

---

Q: What does the principle of superposition state?
A: When two waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is equal to the sum of the displacements of the individual waves.

---

Q: What is constructive interference?
A: When individual displacements are in the same direction and add together to produce a maximum resultant displacement.

---

Q: What is destructive interference?
A: When individual displacements are in opposite directions and counteract each other to produce a minimum resultant displacement.

---

Q: What does it mean for two waves to be coherent?
A: They are emitted with a constant and unchanging phase difference.

---

Q: What phase difference between two coherent waves produces maximum resultant displacement?
A: An even multiple of $\pi$ radians.

---

Q: What phase difference between two coherent waves produces minimum resultant displacement?
A: An odd multiple of $\pi$ radians.

---

Q: How can superposition of sound waves be investigated experimentally?
A: Using two audio signal generators to emit coherent waves, and moving a microphone connected to an oscilloscope parallel to the speakers to detect regions of loud and quiet noise.

---

Q: What is the formula used to determine wavelength in Young's double-slit experiment?
A: $\lambda = \frac{ax}{d}$, where $a$ is the slit separation, $x$ is the fringe spacing, and $d$ is the distance to the screen.

---

Q: In Young's double-slit experiment, what do the alternating bright and dark fringes represent?
A: Bright fringes are maxima from constructive interference, and dark fringes are minima from destructive interference.

---

Q: What is a diffraction grating?
A: A piece of transparent material with many opaque lines scratched into it, creating multiple slits that produce an interference pattern.

---

Q: What is the diffraction grating equation?
A: $d \sin\theta = n\lambda$, where $d$ is the slit spacing, $\theta$ is the angle to the maxima, $n$ is the order of maxima, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength.

---

Q: How do you calculate the slit spacing ($d$) if a diffraction grating is specified as having $N$ lines per metre?
A: $d = \frac{1}{N}$.

---

Q: How are stationary waves formed?
A: By the superposition of two progressive waves with the same frequency and ideally the same amplitude, travelling in opposite directions.

---

Q: What is a node in a stationary wave?
A: A point which always has zero amplitude.

---

Q: What is an antinode in a stationary wave?
A: A point which always has maximum displacement.

---

Q: What is the distance between two adjacent nodes in a stationary wave?
A: Half a wavelength ($\frac{\lambda}{2}$).

---

Q: Do stationary waves transfer energy?
A: No, they store energy.

---

Q: What is the phase difference between two points on a stationary wave separated by $n$ nodes?
A: $180^\circ n$ (or $n\pi$ radians).

---

Q: How is a stationary wave produced in a stretched string?
A: A vibration generator oscillates the string, and the wave reflects at the pulley, causing the incident and reflected waves to superpose.

---

Q: In a stationary wave on a stretched string, what are the boundary conditions at the generator and pulley ends?
A: Both ends are nodes.

---

Q: How is a stationary wave produced with microwaves?
A: A microwave transmitter produces a wave that reflects off a metal plate, and the incident and reflected waves superpose.

---

Q: How is a stationary wave produced with sound in an air-filled tube?
A: A tuning fork produces sound over the end of the tube, and the length is adjusted until the incident and reflected sound waves superpose to form a stationary wave.

---

Q: What are the boundary conditions for a stationary sound wave in a tube open at both ends?
A: There is an antinode at each open end.

---

Q: What are the boundary conditions for a stationary sound wave in a tube open at one end and closed at the other?
A: There is an antinode at the open end and a node at the closed end.

---

Q: What is the fundamental frequency (first harmonic) of a stationary wave?
A: The lowest frequency of vibration for a given arrangement that produces a stationary wave.

---

Q: At what frequencies are stationary waves formed in a given arrangement?
A: Only at multiples of the fundamental frequency (harmonics).
