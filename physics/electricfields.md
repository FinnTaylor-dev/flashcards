---
name = "Electric Fields"
---

Q: What is an electric field?
A: A region of space in which charged particles are subject to an electrostatic force.

---

Q: What happens to a neutral object when a positively charged rod is brought near it?
A: The electric field of the rod attracts electrons in the object, shifting them closer to the rod. This polarizes the object, making the near side negative and the far side positive, resulting in an overall attractive force.

---

Q: What is the shape of the electric field around a point charge?
A: A radial field, resembling the spokes of a wheel.

---

Q: In which direction do electric field lines point for a positive point charge?
A: Outwards, away from the charge.

---

Q: In which direction do electric field lines point for a negative point charge?
A: Inwards, towards the charge.

---

Q: How does the strength of an electric field change with radial distance from a point charge?
A: It decreases with distance, which is indicated by the decreasing density of the electric field lines.

---

Q: How can a spherically charged object (e.g., an ion or a Van de Graaff generator sphere) be modelled in terms of its electric field outside the sphere?
A: As a point charge, with a radial field that decreases in strength with distance away from the surface of the sphere.

---

Q: What are the rules for drawing electric field lines as they exit a surface?
A: They must be equally spaced and perpendicular to the surface.

---

Q: What do electric field lines that stretch from a positive charge to a negative charge represent?
A: Attraction.

---

Q: What do electric field lines that do not join up represent?
A: Repulsion.

---

Q: How is electric field strength ($E$) defined?
A: The electrostatic force ($F$) applied per unit positive charge ($Q$).

---

Q: What is the formula for electric field strength ($E$) in terms of force ($F$) and charge ($Q$)?
A: $E = \frac{F}{Q}$

---

C: The strength of an electric field, $E$, is defined as the [force] applied per unit [charge], expressed as $E = \frac{F}{Q}$.

---

Q: What are the two interchangeable units for electric field strength?
A: N C$^{-1}$ and V m$^{-1}$.

---

Q: Why is electric field strength considered a vector quantity?
A: It has both magnitude (the size of the force experienced per unit charge) and direction (the direction a positive charge would accelerate at that point).

---

Q: State Coulomb's law.
A: Any two point charges exert an electrostatic force between them that is proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

---

C: Coulomb's law states that the electrostatic force between two point charges is proportional to the [product of their charges] and inversely proportional to the [square of the distance] between them.

---

Q: What is the mathematical formula for Coulomb's law?
A: $F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Qq}{r^2}$

---

Q: What is the value of the constant $k$ (or $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$) in Coulomb's law?
A: $8.99 \times 10^9$ N m$^2$ C$^{-2}$.

---

Q: What is the permittivity of free space ($\varepsilon_0$) and its value?
A: It is the permittivity of a vacuum, representing the lowest possible value for permittivity since there is no matter to become polarized. Its value is $8.85 \times 10^{-12}$ F m$^{-1}$.

---

Q: How is the permittivity of a material generally expressed?
A: As the relative permittivity ($\varepsilon_r$) multiplied by the permittivity of free space ($\varepsilon_0$).

---

Q: What is the formula for the electric field strength ($E$) of a point charge ($Q$) at a distance ($r$)?
A: $E = \frac{Q}{4\pi r^2 \varepsilon_0}$

---

Q: What property of an object creates a gravitational field, and what creates an electric field?
A: Mass creates a gravitational field; charge creates an electric field.

---

Q: How does the nature of the force differ between gravitational and electric fields?
A: Gravitational force is always attractive, whereas electric force can be attractive (between opposite charges) or repulsive (between like charges).

---

Q: What is a uniform electric field?
A: An electric field that is constant in magnitude and direction throughout space, typically produced between two parallel oppositely charged plates.

---

Q: How is work done defined in the context of forces?
A: The product of the force and the distance over which the force acts in the direction of the force ($W = Fd$).

---

Q: How is potential difference defined in an electric field?
A: The work done per unit charge in moving a charge from one point to another in the field ($V = \frac{W}{Q}$).

---

Q: What is the electric potential at a single point?
A: The work done per unit charge to bring a positive test charge from infinity to that point.

---

Q: What is the electric potential defined as at infinity?
A: 0.

---

Q: What is the formula for electric field strength ($E$) in a uniform field between parallel plates?
A: $E = \frac{V}{d}$, where $V$ is the potential difference and $d$ is the distance between the plates.

---

C: In a uniform electric field between parallel plates, the electric field strength is given by $E = \frac{V}{d}$, where $V$ is the [potential difference] and $d$ is the [distance between the plates].

---

Q: The motion of a charged particle through a uniform electric field is analogous to what type of motion in a gravitational field?
A: Projectile motion.

---

Q: In Millikan's oil drop experiment, what two forces are balanced when an oil drop hovers in midair?
A: The downward weight force ($mg$) and the upward electric force ($Eq$ or $\frac{Vq}{d}$).

---

Q: What is the equilibrium equation for a hovering oil drop in Millikan's experiment?
A: $mg = \frac{Vq}{d}$

---

Q: How is the charge ($Q$) on an oil drop in Millikan's experiment expressed?
A: As a whole number ($n$) of elementary charges ($e$), i.e., $Q = -ne$.

---

Q: What is the value of the elementary charge ($e$) determined by Millikan's experiment?
A: $1.6022 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

---

Q: What is the formula for the electrical potential energy ($E_e$) of a point charge $q$ at a distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$?
A: $E_e = \frac{Qq}{4\pi r \varepsilon_0}$

---

Q: Why is the electrical potential energy negative when bringing an oppositely charged particle from infinity to a distance $r$?
A: Because the particles attract, kinetic energy is gained as the particle accelerates towards the charge. This means energy must be supplied to separate the particles back to infinity.

---

Q: What is the formula for the electric potential ($V$) at a distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$?
A: $V = \frac{Q}{4\pi r \varepsilon_0}$
