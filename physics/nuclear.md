Q: What was the model of the atom proposed after the discovery of the electron, featuring a uniform sphere of positive charge with embedded electrons?
A: Thomson's Plum Pudding Model.

---

Q: What experiment disproved the Plum Pudding Model?
A: Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment.

---

Q: Why was a vacuum necessary in Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment?
A: To prevent the alpha particles from being stopped by air in the apparatus.

---

Q: What material was used to detect deflected alpha particles in Rutherford's experiment?
A: A ring of scintillators, which are materials that release photons when a particle hits them.

---

Q: What was the expected outcome of Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment based on the Plum Pudding Model?
A: Alpha particles would be deflected to a large degree on average due to the wall of charge in their path.

---

Q: What was the actual observation of alpha particles in Rutherford's scattering experiment?
A: The majority passed straight through with only a slight deflection of about one degree, and a small proportion were deflected by more than 90 degrees.

---

Q: What did Rutherford deduce about the atom's structure from the fact that most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil?
A: The atom is mostly empty space.

---

Q: What did Rutherford deduce about the atom's structure from the fact that a small proportion of alpha particles were deflected by more than 90 degrees?
A: The atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the atom's mass.

---

Q: What is the alternative name for the Rutherford model of the atom?
A: The Planetary model.

---

Q: What are isotopes?
A: Atoms of the same element with differing numbers of neutrons.

---

Q: Why do isotopes undergo the same chemical reactions but different nuclear reactions?
A: Their electronic configurations are identical, but the stability of their nuclei may differ greatly.

---

Q: What is a nucleon?
A: A subatomic particle that resides in the nucleus of an atom, meaning it is either a proton or a neutron.

---

Q: What is the charge of a proton relative to an electron?
A: Equal and opposite, denoted as $+e$.

---

Q: What does the atomic or proton number, $Z$, represent?
A: The number of protons in the nucleus.

---

Q: What does the mass or nucleon number, $A$, represent?
A: The sum of the number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

---

Q: How can the mass of an atom be estimated using the mass number?
A: By multiplying the mass number by the atomic mass unit.

---

Q: How is the atomic mass unit, $1\text{u}$, defined and what is its value in kilograms?
A: It is defined as one twelfth the mass of carbon-12, and its value is $1.661 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$.

---

Q: Why is an atom often heavier than the sum of its constituent parts?
A: Due to mass-energy equivalence and binding energy.

---

Q: What is the approximate radius of a gold nucleus?
A: $10^{-14}\text{ m}$.

---

Q: How was the radius of the gold nucleus estimated in Rutherford's experiment?
A: From the distribution of deflection angles caused by electrostatic repulsion, using Coulomb's law with the known alpha particle charge of $+2e$, energy of $\sim 1.2 \times 10^{-12}\text{ J}$, and gold nucleus charge of $+79e$.

---

Q: What is the approximate radius of an atom and how does it compare to the nucleus?
A: It is of order $10^{-10}\text{ m}$, which is 10,000 times greater than the radius of the nucleus.

---

Q: What is the approximate nuclear density?
A: $10^{17}\text{ kg m}^{-3}$.

---

Q: What is the formula for the radius of a nucleus, $R$, in terms of the relative atomic mass, $A$?
A: $R = r_0 A^{1/3}$

---

Q: What is the approximate value of the constant $r_0$ in the nuclear radius formula?
A: $1.2\text{ fm}$, where $1\text{ fm} = 10^{-15}\text{ m}$.

---

Q: How was the constant $r_0$ in the nuclear radius formula determined?
A: Via electron diffraction experiments, where electrons with known de Broglie wavelengths were directed at thin metal foils.

---

Q: What are the four fundamental forces in the universe?
A: Gravitational, electromagnetic, weak nuclear, and strong nuclear forces.

---

Q: Which fundamental force acts on particles with mass, is always attractive, has an infinite range, but is very weak?
A: The gravitational force.

---

Q: Which fundamental force acts on particles with charge and has an infinite range?
A: The electromagnetic force.

---

Q: Which fundamental force is responsible for beta decay and acts to change quark types over very small distances?
A: The weak nuclear force.

---

Q: Which fundamental force acts between all nucleons and quarks, counteracting repulsive electrostatic forces between protons?
A: The strong nuclear force.

---

Q: What is the range and behavior of the strong nuclear force with respect to distance?
A: It is attractive at small distances up to $\sim 3\text{ fm}$ and repulsive at incredibly small distances below $\sim 0.5\text{ fm}$, with a limited overall range.

---

Q: What is the mass-energy equivalence equation?
A: $\Delta E = \Delta m c^2$

---

Q: What does the mass-energy equivalence principle state?
A: Mass and energy are different manifestations of the same thing; mass can be thought of as energy in the form of mass-energy.

---

Q: What phenomenon clearly demonstrates the concept of mass as a form of energy?
A: The annihilation of matter and antimatter.

---

Q: Why is rest mass considered the only true measure of mass agreed upon by all observers?
A: Because any object given high enough energy can acquire an incredibly high mass due to mass-energy equivalence, making rest mass the only invariant measure.

---

Q: What is binding energy?
A: The minimum energy required to break a nucleus into its constituent components.

---

Q: Why is the mass of a helium atom slightly lower than the mass of its constituent parts?
A: Because the binding energy has increased, and the mass must decrease to obey mass-energy conservation.

---

Q: How is binding energy typically quoted to allow comparison between different nuclei?
A: Per nucleon.

---

Q: What is mass defect in the context of nuclear synthesis?
A: The difference in mass between the constituent nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.

---

Q: Which isotope is the most stable due to having the maximum binding energy per nucleon?
A: Iron-56.

---

Q: What happens to the binding energy per nucleon for low nucleon numbers where $A < 56$?
A: It increases.

---

Q: What is the process called when two small nuclei combine, resulting in a mass defect and energy release?
A: Fusion.

---

Q: Why does fusion release energy for nuclei with $A < 56$?
A: Due to a significant increase in the strong attraction between nucleons compared to the small increase in electrostatic repulsion.

---

Q: What happens to the binding energy per nucleon for high nucleon numbers where $A > 56$?
A: It decreases.

---

Q: What is the process called when a large nucleus is broken apart into two smaller daughter nuclei, releasing energy?
A: Fission.

---

Q: Why does fission release energy for nuclei with $A > 56$?
A: Due to the large proton number causing a large repulsive electrostatic force and a relatively feeble strong attraction from the increased average distance between nucleons.

---

Q: What are the properties of a particle and its corresponding antiparticle?
A: They have equal mass but opposite charge.

---

Q: What happens when a particle and its antiparticle meet?
A: They annihilate to produce energy in the form of photons.

---

Q: What is pair production?
A: The process where a high-energy photon spontaneously creates a matter-antimatter pair.

---

Q: What is the minimum energy required for a photon to create an electron-positron pair?
A: $1.02\text{ MeV}$, since the rest mass of both the electron and positron is $\sim 0.51\text{ MeV}/c^2$.

---

Q: Why are particle rest masses often quoted in units of $\text{MeV}/c^2$?
A: Because the photon creating the pair must have an energy greater than the combined rest masses of the two particles, as dictated by mass-energy equivalence.

---

Q: What are the two main classes of fundamental particles?
A: Hadrons and leptons.

---

Q: What are hadrons made of, and which forces act on them?
A: They are made of quarks and are acted on by both the strong and weak nuclear forces.

---

Q: What are the two classes of hadrons based on their quark composition?
A: Mesons, which are quark-antiquark pairs, and baryons, which are groups of three quarks.

---

Q: Give two examples of baryons.
A: Protons and neutrons.

---

Q: Give an example of a meson.
A: Pions.

---

Q: What is the baryon number of a baryon and an antibaryon?
A: Baryons have a baryon number of 1, and antibaryons have a baryon number of -1.

---

Q: What fractional charges do quarks possess?
A: $\pm 1/3$ or $\pm 2/3$ of the elementary charge.

---

Q: What is the quark composition and charge of a proton?
A: Two up quarks and one down quark, resulting in a charge of $+1e$.

---

Q: What is the quark composition and charge of a neutron?
A: Two down quarks and one up quark, resulting in a neutral charge of 0.

---

Q: What is the quark composition and charge of an antiproton?
A: Two anti-up quarks and one anti-down quark, resulting in a charge of $-1e$.

---

Q: What conservation rules apply to particle collisions?
A: Conservation of mass-energy, momentum, charge, baryon number, and lepton number.

---

Q: What property must be conserved in strong interactions involving hadrons with strange quarks?
A: Strangeness.

---

Q: What is the strangeness value of a hadron containing a strange quark, and one containing an anti-strange quark?
A: A strange quark gives a strangeness of -1, and an anti-strange quark gives a strangeness of +1.

---

Q: When is strangeness not conserved?
A: When a hadron decays via the weak force.

---

Q: What are leptons?
A: Fundamental particles that are not affected by the strong force but are subject to the weak nuclear force.

---

Q: Give four examples of leptons.
A: Electrons, positrons, neutrinos, and muons.

---

Q: What is the lepton number of a lepton and an antilepton?
A: Leptons have a lepton number of 1, and antileptons have a lepton number of -1.

---

Q: Why are neutrinos difficult to detect?
A: They have a mass very close to zero and no charge, so they rarely interact with matter.

---

Q: What is radioactive decay?
A: The spontaneous breakdown of an atomic nucleus resulting in the release of energy and matter.

---

Q: Why is radioactive decay described as a random process?
A: Because it is impossible to predict which specific identical nucleus will decay next.

---

Q: What is the half-life of a radioactive sample?
A: The average time taken for half of the active isotopes in the sample to decay.

---

Q: What are the three main forms of radiation?
A: Alpha particles, beta particles, and gamma rays.

---

Q: What are the properties of an alpha particle regarding mass, charge, speed, ionising ability, penetrating power, and stopping material?
A: Mass is $4\text{u}$, charge is $+2$, speed is slow, ionising ability is high, penetrating power is low, and it is stopped by paper.

---

Q: What are the properties of a beta particle regarding mass, charge, speed, ionising ability, penetrating power, and stopping material?
A: Mass is $1/2000\text{u}$, charge is $-1$, speed is fast, ionising ability is medium, penetrating power is medium, and it is stopped by aluminium.

---

Q: What are the properties of a gamma ray regarding mass, charge, speed, ionising ability, penetrating power, and stopping material?
A: Mass is 0, charge is 0, speed is the speed of light, ionising ability is none, penetrating power is high, and it is stopped by lead.

---

Q: How can different types of radiation be distinguished experimentally?
A: By screening the source with different materials and measuring the drop in activity, or by observing their different responses to magnetic and electric fields.

---

Q: What is a bubble chamber?
A: A radiation detector that uses tanks of water to form tracks of bubbles when ionising particles pass through them.

---

Q: What is a Geiger-Muller tube?
A: A radiation detector that forms cascades of electrons when an ionising particle hits the detector.

---

Q: What causes the transformation of quarks in beta decay?
A: The weak nuclear force, via the emission of leptons.

---

Q: What happens at the quark level during beta-minus decay?
A: A down quark mutates into an up quark, transforming a neutron into a proton.

---

Q: What particles are released during beta-minus decay to conserve charge and lepton number?
A: An electron, also known as a beta particle, and an electron antineutrino.

---

Q: Write the nuclear equation for beta-minus decay of a neutron.
A: $n_0^1 \rightarrow p_1^1 + e_{-1}^0 + \bar{\nu}_e$

---

Q: What happens at the quark level during beta-plus decay?
A: An up quark transforms into a down quark, changing a proton into a neutron.

---

Q: What particles are released during beta-plus decay to conserve charge and lepton number?
A: A positron and an electron neutrino.

---

Q: Write the nuclear equation for beta-plus decay of a proton.
A: $p_1^1 \rightarrow n_0^1 + e_{+1}^0 + \nu_e$

---

Q: What is alpha decay?
A: The loss of an alpha particle, which is a helium nucleus, from a very unstable nucleus.

---

Q: Why does alpha emission occur?
A: It can be thought of as spontaneous fission where the strong nuclear force is not great enough to overcome electrostatic repulsion between protons.

---

Q: Write the general nuclear equation for alpha decay.
A: $X_Z^A \rightarrow Y_{Z-2}^{A-4} + \alpha_2^4$

---

Q: What is a decay chain?
A: A succession of nuclear decays that occur until a stable atom is reached.

---

Q: Why do stable nuclei with large proton numbers have more neutrons than protons?
A: More nucleons are needed to produce enough strong force attraction to counteract the rising electromagnetic repulsive force between protons.

---

Q: What type of decay are nuclei with greater than 82 protons more likely to undergo?
A: Alpha decay.

---

Q: What type of decay are proton-rich nuclei, located to the right of the belt of stability, more likely to undergo?
A: Beta-plus decay.

---

Q: What type of decay are neutron-rich nuclei, located to the left of the belt of stability, more likely to undergo?
A: Beta-minus decay.

---

Q: What causes gamma decay?
A: A nucleus having surplus energy following alpha or beta emission.

---

Q: Does gamma decay change the nucleon composition of a nucleus?
A: No, it only releases energy in the form of a gamma photon.

---

Q: What is the activity of a radioactive source?
A: The rate at which nuclei decay, or the number of decays per second.

---

Q: What is the unit of activity?
A: Becquerel, denoted as $\text{Bq}$ or $\text{s}^{-1}$.

---

Q: What is the relationship between activity $A$, decay constant $\lambda$, and the number of active nuclei $N$?
A: $A = \lambda N$

---

Q: What is the decay constant, $\lambda$?
A: The probability that an individual nucleus will decay per unit time.

---

Q: Why must background radiation be deducted from measured activity?
A: Because background radiation from cosmic rays, natural compounds, and anthropogenic sources increases the measured activity, giving a false reading of the sample's true activity.

---

Q: What is the differential equation representing the rate of change of unstable nuclei over time?
A: $\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N$

---

Q: What is the integrated form of the radioactive decay equation for the number of nuclei, $N$, at time $t$?
A: $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$

---

Q: What is the integrated form of the radioactive decay equation for activity, $A$, at time $t$?
A: $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$

---

Q: What is the relationship between half-life $t_{1/2}$ and the decay constant $\lambda$?
A: $\lambda t_{1/2} = \ln(2)$, which can be rearranged to $t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda}$

---

Q: How can the decay constant be determined from a graph of activity versus time?
A: By plotting $\ln(A)$ versus $t$, which gives a straight line with a gradient equal to $-\lambda$.

---

Q: What are the three main isotopes of naturally occurring carbon in the atmosphere?
A: C-12, C-13, and C-14.

---

Q: Why does the ratio of C-14 to C-12 in a living organism match the atmospheric ratio?
A: Because living organisms continuously absorb C-14 through photosynthesis or consumption of other organisms.

---

Q: What happens to the amount of C-14 in an organism at the point of death?
A: The number of C-14 atoms is capped and begins to decay via beta emission with a half-life of $\sim 5700$ years.

---

Q: What are the limitations of radiocarbon dating?
A: It assumes the atmospheric C-12 to C-14 ratio has remained constant, background radiation can interfere with small samples, and it cannot be used for samples much older than 5700 years as the C-14 becomes immeasurably small.

---

Q: What element is often used for dating samples much older than 5700 years?
A: Rubidium, due to its longer half-life.

---

Q: What is nuclear fusion?
A: The process where small nuclei are fused together to produce larger nuclei and energy.

---

Q: What must nuclei overcome to fuse together, and what conditions are required to achieve this?
A: They must overcome electrostatic repulsion, which requires high temperatures and pressures.

---

Q: What is the proton-proton chain?
A: The stellar fusion process in main sequence stars like the sun where lone protons fuse to form unstable helium-2, which undergoes beta decay to form deuterium, eventually fusing further to form helium-4.

---

Q: What is the CNO cycle?
A: The predominant fusion process in larger stars, in which carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen are synthesised.

---

Q: What is supernova nucleosynthesis?
A: The process where the heaviest elements are synthesized by fusion during a violent supernova at the end of a massive star's life.

---

Q: What is a major advantage of artificial fusion reactors?
A: They have no radioactive by-products.

---

Q: What is a major challenge of artificial fusion reactors?
A: The extremely high temperatures and pressures needed to maintain fusion can currently only be operated for short periods of time.

---

Q: What is nuclear fission?
A: The breaking apart of large nuclei into smaller nuclei, causing a reduction in total binding energy, a mass defect, and a release of energy.

---

Q: Why is uranium-235 typically used as fissile material?
A: Because it easily undergoes fission and is relatively abundant.

---

Q: What happens when a low-speed thermal neutron is fired at a uranium-235 nucleus?
A: It absorbs the neutron to become the unstable uranium-236 isotope, which then either decays via fission, splitting into two smaller daughter nuclei and more fast neutrons, or decays via gamma emission.

---

Q: Where does the total energy released in fission come from?
A: The combination of the kinetic energy of the neutrons and daughter nuclei, and the energy of the photons, resulting from the mass defect.

---

Q: What is the function of the thermal coolant in a fission reactor?
A: It absorbs thermal energy from the fission process and transforms it into kinetic energy in turbines, which turn a generator to produce electrical energy.

---

Q: What is the function of the moderator in a fission reactor?
A: It slows down the high-energy neutrons released by fission, increasing the chance they will cause further uranium-235 fission reactions.

---

Q: Give two examples of moderator materials.
A: Water and graphite.

---

Q: What is the function of control rods in a fission reactor?
A: They absorb thermal neutrons to prevent them from causing further fission reactions, thereby controlling the rate of the reaction.

---

Q: Give two examples of control rod materials.
A: Boron and cadmium.

---

Q: Why is radioactive waste from fission reactors hazardous for millennia?
A: Because products like Plutonium-239 have long half-lives, such as $\sim 24,000$ years.

---

Q: How is long-lived radioactive waste typically disposed of?
A: It is buried deep underground in locations designed to be safe from attack and protected against earthquakes.
